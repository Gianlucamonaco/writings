## Objectives and approach

This text represents an attempt to develop a personal writing practice, and it will be based on two seemingly divergent approaches. On the one hand, scientific validity is sought, in the sense that it will be grounded on an academic structure and literature. On the other hand, this text aims to challenge scientific writing through formal, conceptual and content decisions. I come from a background in design and classical music, but most of the knowledge I have acquired to develop my professional and artistic practice comes from self-study. From my experience, I find that pushing the boundaries of a new discipline is usually the most effective strategy for understanding and engaging critically. From the use of language in footnotes to general layout choices, I hope that this critical approach will emerge in this short essay and influence my future writing practice.

This text is written in the context of the intro seminar cycle of the Transdisciplinarity Masters at ZHdK. A first draft of this text was conceived before engaging with any of the seminar readings. As the seminar progressed, many of the topics and perspectives proved to be insightful for the development of this text. I will now briefly mention the main authors cited in this text, as well as the concepts from their work that resonated most.

Katherine Busch contests the idea of **science as the highest and truest form of knowledge**, insisting that science it is not neutral and it exerts pressure on how knowledge should or should not be conceived. In line with this thinking, Marisol de la Cadena questions **scientific binaries and the partitioning of the sensible** within Western culture, contrasting them with alternative forms of understanding reality. I found a fruitful tension between Jürgen Mittelstrass, who claims that **subjects, theories and methods used in research are never exclusive to a discipline**, and Ludwik Fleck, who theorizes **the impossibility of communication without compromises** between thought collectives. Fleck also argues that **language determines what can be thought**, a concept that takes a metaphysical connotation when interpreted in the light of Federico Campagna’s notion of “absolute language”. I will discuss all these points of view, in light of which I will venture to formulate a *theory of models*.

From the juxtaposition of the positions of these scholars, the following questions emerged:

- Is it necessary to develop a clear thesis and provide answers, in order to produce meaningful discourses and contribute to knowledge?
- Can challenging the standards (e.g. footnotes, digressions, layout) play an active role in the development of the discourse?
- Can different versions of the same text (with their marginal or discarded contents) resonate into the final form of a text?
- Can I theorize an overarching system that sheds a different light on the relationship between science and other forms of knowledge?

I will try to demonstrate my hypoteses through the form and content of this text. I have to admit, however, my reluctance towards certain experimental layouts which, to my eyes, act more as a hindrance (or a seductive trap, depends on the perspective), than supporting the positions of the text. I might even hate the final result of this text, who knows.

As for the process, I will use GitHub as a versioning control tool to keep track of the progress, for each work session. This approach will allow me not only to maintain a record of my writing process, but also to make informed writing decisions.

## What is the purpose of science?

This question is far too broad, and perhaps beyond the scope of the text. In the context of this text, the primary goal is to define boundaries and establish a dialogue between what lies within and what lies outside of the scientific realm, in order to identify and to address any points of friction. I will try to provide some coordinates and discuss a few divergent positions. Scientific research is often oriented towards discovery through observation, definition and rational argumentation, in order to expand the realm of knowledge, to understand and describe natural and social phenomena`[1]`. Effective scientific development is characterized by a process guided by logos, the formulation of methods, and the definition of conventions. These are essential factors for the scientific community, as they facilitate communication and sharing processes.

> Let’s do now a cinematic thought experiment: we start by observing science in its general sense. At this level usually happens the discourse on science as a single, vast field of inquiry. We begin to gradually zoom in, as a scientist would with a microscope. Our first encounter would probably be the divide between “soft” and “hard” sciences. Already at this point, we realize that the structure that we're observing is the result of a historical process (Mittelstrass, 2018), and that it hasn't always been this way. As we keep zooming in, several disciplines would start to emerge: very different in size, some would appear as the extension of others, some would come in some nebulous, funny, complex, mysterious form. If we proceed further, we would observe how disciplines are distributed across several institutions, and the institutions formed by groups of people. People would mostly act within the boundaries of their discipline, but, from time to time, they would be attempt to cross those boundaries—at their own risk, of course. Now stop there. We are conceptually at the scale of individual human beings.

I would like us to keep this still frame in our brain, while we focus on the words of Bill Readings: ≪Communication cannot be the transfer of a prefabricated meaning, since the meaning of words does not remain the same from one utterance—or more precisely, one idiolect—to the next.≫ (Readings, 1996). What I particularly appreciate about this quote is that it not only reflects the flexible nature of the human ability to attribute meanings to things; it also describes how large language models currently work. We will elaborate how technology represents a cognitive constraint further on in this text, but it is worth noting that, even for a machine, the meaning of words does not derive from their definition, but rather from their situatedness. + **Assuming that it is not possible to convey the exact same meaning in the communication between two individuals, how can we expect effective communication to occur on a larger scale, across (trans-) disciplines?**

> Cut.`[16:9]`

In his analysis of the concepts of disciplinarity, interdisciplinarity and transdisciplinarity, Jürgen Mittelstrass argues that the effects of scientific research always transcend disciplinary boundaries, since it always addresses problems that affect the world (Mittelstrass, 2018). This pragmatic and utilitarian view of science is found not only among scientists, but rationality also has positive connotations in society at large`[?1]`.
By extending this mode of thinking to every aspect of human experience, non-rational thinking, intuition and belief acquire a negative connotation. When a need for rigor and clarity go far beyond scientific realm, they can become objects of veneration, and Reason permeates into every aspect of life, becoming the only possible path to pursuing a virtuous existence.

> Let’s do another little thought experiment. Pick someone close to you. Imagine they told you they supported what you believed to be a conspiracy theory. How would you react? Would you warn them of the danger? Would you try to convince them that their positions are unfounded? What if there were actually something to learn from them? What if you were the one in danger? Is it possible that such theories are a byproduct of the persistent belief that reason is the only way to explain reality?

The problem that I am addressing here is not about who is right or wrong. Scientific research has contributed immensely to the human progress, but having only one lens through which we interpret the world, communicate, and assign truth can be problematic, as it can reduce every aspect of life to binary dichotomies. + **So, how can we not fall in the single-mode-of-thinking trap?**

Here I suggest an alternative perspective on science, both historically and conceptually. In his work Human all too human, Friedrich Nietzsche discusses the conflictual relationship between science and non-science. First of all, he believes that science is a source of greater pleasure for those who work with it than for those who learn from it. But the nature of this pleasure is ephemeral, and gradually fades as soon as scientific truths enter the everyday life. Furthermore, science casts suspicion on other disciplines, with the effect of impoverishing human experience in general. As a countermeasure, he advocates the advent of a higher form of culture, capable of providing a dual brain: one for experiencing science, and one for non-science (Nietzsche, 1878). This position is questionable on many levels: first, it entails a clear separation between what belongs to the scientific sphere and what does not; second, it promotes the need for hierarchy and power through the idea of a higher culture. Nonetheless, the philosopher warns us against the conception of science as the sole source of truth–what we could define a scientific hegemony–and opens up the possibility of other enriching ways of experiencing the world.

In the final chapter dedicated to my *theory of models,* I will attempt to propose a conceptual framework for reflecting about scientific and non-scientific ways of experiencing the world. But first, we need to investigate why the notion of binary thinking is limiting, and thus deconstruct the opposition between scientific and non-scientific.

## Critique to binary thinking

Every scientific discipline is characterized by a technical language and standardized methodologies and practices. This approach ensures efficiency and consistency within the scientific community. As we saw in our thought experiment, disciplines as we know them are not “natural” or “god given”, but their development and boundaries are rooted in history (Mittelstrass, 2018). Each specific language is by no means absolute, although it remains essential to the proper development of the relative discipline.

While it may appear to be a useful and desirable solution, this text rejects the possibility of an absolute and universal language capable of bridging disciplines. This point is probably obvious and irrelevant to mention, but it seems to me that, especially in general contexts, science has often the power of deciding what is true without any doubt. If there’s scientific evidence, well… it is better to listen to it. I am pretty sure that you could find plenty of examples your daily life.`[4]`

Viewing every aspect of the world through a single lens (the techno-scientific one is probably the most widespread today), leaves no room for ambiguity and indeterminacy. Language and grammar play a fundamental role in creating purpose and meaning, and can profoundly shape structures and affect thoughts and behaviours of different communities. In a world where only one type of language is possible, this turns into a powerful means of control. `[?2]`

The philosopher Federico Campagna describes the world as we know it today as the world of Technic, where “absolute language” is the governing principle. In this view, the reality-system of Technic is generated as a “catalogue of cuts”. Measurement and cutting represent an infinitely replicable process through which language can inscribe and disintegrate reality as such (Campagna, 2018). **+** **The problem is not thinking in terms of binary oppositions *per sé*, but how a system of beliefs that is based on binary structures becomes absolute and tolerates no alternative**. Far from being a political call to arms, the point of the Italian philosopher is to remind us that reality-systems are built on metaphysical axioms, and that their modification is always possible.

Another perspective on the world-making process and binary oppositions is offered by Marisol de la Cadena in her text *Uncommoning Nature*. Through the lens of the *anthropo-not-seen*, the author sheds a light on the effects of the divide between the human and non-human, which Western politics had always an interest to reinforce. The practices of many non-western populations, from the Awajun-Wampis in Peru to the Mapuche in Argentina, reveal forms of co-dependency between humans and nature that are entangled ≪in such a way that pulling them apart would transform them into something else≫ (de la Cadena, 2015).

To counter an ever-growing techno-scientific hegemony, it is essential to embrace multiple reality-systems at once, to learn how to be in the world in different ways at the same time.

## Beyond simulation / Technological delusion

In the previous chapters I have presented the dangers of techno-scientific hegemony and the consequences of binary thinking in general terms. I will try to elaborate further in the context of digital technologies.

The common perception around technology today is controversial to say the least. On the one hand, humanity has achieved extraordinary results in communication, research and production through technological development. On the other hand, it is evident that the consequences of technology can be deeply problematic both on the individual and collective sphere. When did we start to see the negative effects of digital technologies on a global scale? In one of his last essays entitled `Transdisciplinarity Must Become Transversality`, Felix Guattari manifests a certain sympathy for technology, or at least he places some hope in it. Envisioning a future of transdisciplinarity strongly connected with the development of new technologies, the author states:

> In the first place, it will be possible to liberate a greater and greater quantity of activity and of labour from repetitive material tasks and to devote it to study, research and culture, which will establish new junctions of all kinds between themselves.`[<3]` […] In the second place,the evolution of informatics, its junction with television, telematics, data banks and the image, will develop a sort of machinic transdisciplinarity. This is already largely the case with every major discovery, every major technological innovation, which does not just irrigate neighbouring domains but frequently has fallout in the most distant of domains. […] In the third place, one might think that the era to come will lay down a profound transformation of the means of expression, knowledge, coordination and sensibility.
>
> *(Guattari, 1992)*

We can certainly understand the French intellectual's positions and justify his enthusiasm if we consider the historical context in which they were conceived, but we cannot ignore what has happened in the last thirty years to draw our very different conclusions. **At what point did an optimistic vision of the future transform into a narrative that promotes techno-scientific hegemony? Was there ever a transition, or are these simply two sides of the same coin?**

While a big part of the public debate about technology focuses on political, ecological, social, ethical issues—all extremely valid and urgent—developing a sensitivity to a metaphysical conception of reality could provide solid foundations for developing long-term strategies, both for individuals and society as a whole. Many philosophers are active in the current discourse on technology and AI, addressing the many ethical questions that have emerged in the recent years, but in many cases their effort serves a technological purpose. My interest is here not so much on how we make our technology better, safer, more accessible, and more equitable, but rather in **how we conceive, perceive, and act in the world, taking into account our inevitable exposure to culture, language, technologies.**

For example, the act of converting the sensible (images, sound, text, …) into digital formats implies a necessary reduction, compression and loss of information. While it might be tempting to ignore the parts we consider irrelevant and negligible (science does this all the time), there might be something essential hidden in those tiny details. Hito Steyerl refers to the imagery generated through AI as “mean images”, in all the possible meanings the word “mean” implies. Generated images have lost their indexical reference to their object, but they converge around what is average, they are functions of average stochastic values (Steyerl, 2023).

Another argument could be that the human brain is constantly selecting and interpreting external inputs, and thus a reduction of information is necessary for our performing our living functions. But the machine still lies between us and the sensible, an invisible filter that predigests and preselects everything, and then our ability to digest and select becomes slowly dependent on that external entity.

The ever-growing pervasiveness of digital technologies in our lives has a fundamental problem: it reinforces a single perspective on reality. What makes it even worse, is the fact that we don’t perceive any shift at all. By being constantly exposed, and by compulsively translating every aspect of our life into digital form, we are convinced to believe that everything can be translated, that it is just a matter of technique and architecture. The belief that a human brain can be reproduced—not just simulated—together with the idea that any sensible or mental phenomenon can be reduced to a series of logical steps, implies that we can understand, control and manipulate virtually *anything*. But no matter how powerful, granular or accurate, the current information technology is based on one simple principle: divide and conquer. **+** The illusion of seamlesness represents a potential danger, as **it introduces a cognitive dissonance between what we perceive** (a smooth, ultra-detailed, closest-to-nature experience) **and what it actually is** (a ridiculous amount of numbers and operations that materialize as a copy of the sensible).

By questioning science and digital technologies, my aim is to reveal the boundaries, the limits and the axioms implicit in the discoveries and methods. **If it is not possible to convey the exact same meaning in the communication between two individuals, how can we expect effective communication to occur on a larger scale, across (trans-) disciplines?**

Ludwik Fleck will help us to elaborate on the problem from a language perspective. Similarly to Bill Readings, he theorizes the impossibility of a pure, *lossless* exchange of ideas from the perspective of *thought collectives*. It is not only a matter of socio-cultural friction between groups: language itself represents the main obstacle. *Thought styles* constrain ≪the individual by determining "what can be thought in no other way."≫. He continues: ≪Since all words bear a more or less distinctive coloring conforming to a given thought style, a character which changes during their passage from one collective to the next, they always undergo a certain change in their meaning as they circulate intercollectively. One could compare the meaning of the words “force,” “energy,” or “experiment” for a physicist, a philologist or a sportsman≫ (Fleck, 1935).

Dwelling on this apparent contradiction inherent in language, we can conclude that not all differences, oppositions, contrasts need to be resolved. **+** **Language is a means to define, to sharpen, to separate and—*at the same time*—an intangible, poetic, open-ended device**.

In a similar fashion, the notion of transdisciplinarity entails the dissolution of boundaries between the disciplines, while–*at the same time*–requiring them. A transdisciplinary process sacrifices the rigor required by the discipline in order to gain a *different type* of understanding. In this sense, transdisciplinarity does not present itself as a better substitute for estabilished disciplines; rather, it encourages a constant questioning and renegotiation of the discipline structures, as well as creating space for transformation and the emergence of new fields of inquiry.`[0]`

## Theory of models

As I stated in the introduction, the goal of the theory of models is to theorize an overarching system that sheds a different light on the relationship between science and other forms of knowledge. By a different light I mean:

a. different from a hierarchical vision that imposes that some views are better or more relevant than others (which I believe to be a common fact).
b. different as it does not entail clear definitions and boundaries between science and other forms of understanding the world (what I call the models).

What do we mean by understanding the world? Is it a single one or there are multiple worlds? Mittelstrass claims that ≪The scientific world is not a closed world, for which the well-known metaphor of the ivory tower stands, but it is – whether science likes it or not – an open world≫ (Mittelstrass, 2018). In my understanding of this extract, the author is trying to say that not only scientists are entitled to contribute to scientific discoveries, and therefore to understand the world. But I would also expect that such contribution happens through—at least partially—the scientific means, methodologies, languages, channels, etc. This is not the raw world (whatever it means), but rather a scientific model of the world. And this is where it starts to become blurry. Where does a model begins and end? Does a model always apply to the whole world? Can a model include other models? Can things cease to be part of a model? Can anyone define what a certain model is? Or do we all have our own models?

`what is a model` By the term model I intend a set of epistemological assumptions and practices that exist in any subject. Science, philosophy, religion and theories in general can be viewed as models that we use to understand or explain the world. We make sense of the world through our models. We project a model onto the world we experience. Multiple models can coexist simultaneously in an individual (and often they do).

`accuracy` The extent to which a model defines and incorporates the world is highly variable. It usually applies to the whole existence, but no model can explain it all. There are always zones of light and shadow. Some tend toward exactness and clear partitioning, while others might be more loose. Both approaches serve specific purposes.

`simultaneity` Each model gives its meaning to things according to their structure, but sometimes models overlap. it is hard to conceive multiple models simultaneously. When you sacrifice sense, you get ambivalence; when you sacrifice truth, you get paradox; when you sacrifice understanding, you get something-I-don’t-have-a-word-for.

`language` Every model is grounded in language (or languages), and therefore it requires a certain degree of approximation and compromise. Even though they are fuzzy and, models have boundaries, and axioms that provide the basic components required to build a system that makes sense. Language makes certain model structures possible. It is not only about the words used and their definition, but also how is a certain meaning assigned, what is its effect and what are boundaries and relationships between words within a certain model.

`limits` It is not possible to explain a model from within. Boundaries can be extended, but transformed, never destroyed. The description of reality cannot be identical to reality itself.

Unconditional adherence to a single model, seen as the “true” or “only possible” description of reality, is problematic. A model may work exceptionally well and suit a specific context, but its partial effectiveness does not demonstrate its validity in absolute terms. This does imply that the more models, the merrier. Embracing all models at once does not lead to an ultimate understanding of the world. Applying multiple models means also encountering contraddictions. We probably cannot even handle it mentally.

So what can we do? We can learn to live with it, we find our own strategies to use them with care and according to the context, we keep ourselves open to other models we don’t understand.
 It’s a bit like going to the gym: you need to exercise constantly to keep yourself in shape. But who cares if you skip it sometimes?
 It’s a bit like surfing: you will always find yourself in conditions you cannot replicate. Isn’t it exciting?
 And, you know what? I think I said already too much.
