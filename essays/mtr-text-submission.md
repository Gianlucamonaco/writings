## Objectives and approach

This text represents an attempt to develop a personal writing practice, and it will be based on two seemingly divergent approaches. On the one hand, scientific validity is sought, in the sense that it will be grounded on an academic structure and literature. On the other hand, this text aims to challenge scientific writing through formal, conceptual and content decisions. I come from a background in design and classical music, but most of the knowledge I have acquired to develop my professional and artistic practice comes from self-study. From my experience, I find that pushing the boundaries of a new discipline is usually the most effective strategy for understanding and engaging critically.

This text is written in the context of the intro seminar cycle of the Transdisciplinarity Masters at ZHdK. Although a first draft of this text was conceived before engaging with any of the seminar readings, many topics and perspectives proved to be aligned with the intent of this text. Therefore, I will briefly mention the main authors cited in this text, as well as the concepts from their work that resonated most.

Katherine Busch contests the idea of **science as the highest and truest form of knowledge**, insisting that science it is not neutral and it exerts pressure on how knowledge should or should not be conceived. In line with this thinking, Marisol de la Cadena questions **scientific binaries and the partitioning of the sensible** within Western culture, contrasting them with alternative forms of understanding reality. I found a fruitful tension between Jürgen Mittelstrass, who claims that **subjects, theories and methods used in research are never exclusive to a discipline**, and Ludwik Fleck, who theorizes **the impossibility of communication without compromises** between thought collectives. Fleck also argues that **language determines what can be thought**, a concept that takes a metaphysical connotation when interpreted in the light of Federico Campagna’s notion of “absolute language”. I will discuss all these points of view, in light of which I will venture to formulate a *theory of models*.

To summarize, this text aims to address the following questions:

- Is it necessary to develop a clear thesis and provide answers, in order to produce meaningful discourses and contribute to knowledge?
- Can challenging the standards (e.g. footnotes, digressions, layout) play an active role in the development of the discourse?
- Can different versions of the same text (with their marginal or discarded contents) resonate into the final form of a text?
- Can I theorize an overarching system that sheds a different light on the relationship between science and other forms of knowledge?

I will try to demonstrate my hypoteses through the form and content of this text. I have to admit, however, my reluctance towards certain experimental layouts which, to my eyes, act more as a hindrance (or a seductive trap, depends on the perspective), than supporting the positions of the text. I might even hate the final result of this text, who knows.

As for the process, I will use GitHub as a versioning control tool to keep track of the progress, for each work session. This approach will allow me not only to maintain a record of my writing process, but also to make informed writing decisions.

## What is the purpose of science?

Scientific research is often oriented towards discovery through definitions and rational argumentations`[1]`, in order to expand the realm of knowledge, to understand and describe natural and social phenomena. Proceeding through the means of the *logos*, formulating methods and defining conventions is key for effective scientific development. If we take a closer look, each discipline has its own rules and specialised language. Those are fundamental for who is within the field, as they make the communication and sharing processes smooth.

As we zoom in, we could see science divided into disciplines; disciplines distributed across several institutions; institutions formed by groups of people. Now stop there. We are conceptually at the scale of individual human beings.

> I would like us to keep this still frame in our brain, while we focus on the words of Bill Readings: ≪Communication cannot be the transfer of a prefabricated meaning, since the meaning of words does not remain the same from one utterance—or more precisely, one idiolect—to the next.≫ (Readings, 1996). + **If it is not possible to convey the exact same meaning in the communication between two individuals, how can we expect effective communication to occur on a larger scale, across (trans-) disciplines?**

In his analysis of the concepts of disciplinarity, interdisciplinarity and transdisciplinarity, Jürgen Mittelstrass argues that the effects of scientific research always transcend disciplinary boundaries, since it always addresses problems that affect the world (Mittelstrass, 2018). This pragmatic and utilitarian view of science is found not only among scientists, but rationality also has positive connotations in society at large`[?1]`. For some individuals, irrational practices or thoughts are seen as negative or as potential threats. In some cases, rigor and clarity that go beyond scientific realm can become objects of veneration, and Reason permeates into every aspect of life, becoming the only possible path to pursuing a virtuous existence.

> [        ] Let’s do another little thought experiment. Pick someone close to you. Imagine they told you they supported what you believed to be a conspiracy theory. How would you react? Would you try to warn them of the danger? Would you try to convince them that their positions were unfounded? What if there were actually something to learn from them? Is it possible that such theories are a byproduct of the persistent belief that reason is the only way to explain reality?

To jump back and reconnect to our discourse: **how can we avoid reducing all this to a simple binary question, in which one simply chooses which side to take?**

We might perhaps glimpse a direction in the words of Friedrich Nietzsche, when he discusses the conflictual relationship between science and non-science in his *Human all too human*. Firstly, he believes that science is a source of greater pleasure for those who work with it than for those who learn from it. But the nature of this pleasure is ephemeral, and gradually fades as soon as scientific truths enter the everyday life. Furthermore, science casts suspicion on other disciplines, with the effect of impoverishing human experience in general. As a countermeasure, higher forms of culture should aim for a dual brain: one for experiencing science, and one for non-science (Nietzsche, 1878). Although this position relies on a clear separation between what belongs to the scientific sphere and what does not, the philosopher warns us against the conception of science as the sole source of truth and opens the way to other ways of experiencing the world.

In the final chapter dedicated to my *theory of models,* I will attempt to propose a conceptual framework for reflecting about scientific and non-scientific ways of experiencing the world. But first, we need to investigate why the notion of binary thinking is limiting, and thus deconstruct the opposition between scientific and non-scientific.

## Critique to binary thinking

We have discussed how every scientific discipline is characterized by technical language and standardized methodologies and practices. This approach ensures efficiency and consistency within the scientific community. Mittelstrass reminds us that the disciplines as we know them are not ‘natural’ or ‘god given’, but their development and boundaries are rooted in history (Mittelstrass, 2018). This is an important point to resisting the temptation to filter every aspect of the world through a single techno-scientific lens, leaving no room for ambiguity and indeterminacy.

The philosopher Federico Campagna describes the world as we know it today as the world of Technic, where “absolute language” is the governing principle. In this view, the reality-system of Technic is generated as a “catalogue of cuts”. Measurement and cutting represent an infinitely replicable process through which language can inscribe and disintegrate reality as such (Campagna, 2018). Far from being a political call to arms, the point of the Italian philosopher is to remind us that reality-systems are built on metaphysical axioms, and that their modification is always possible. The problem is not thinking in terms of binary oppositions per sé, but how a system of beliefs that is based on binary structures becomes absolute and tolerates no alternative.

In contrast, Marisol de la Cadena offers another perspective on the world-making process and binary oppositions in her text *Uncommoning Nature*. Through the lens of the *anthropo-not-seen*, the goal of the author is to shed a light on the effects of the divide between the human and non-human, which Western politics were interested to reinforce. The practices of many non-western populations, from the Awajun-Wampis in Peru to the Mapuche in Argentina, reveal forms of co-dependency between humans and nature that are entangled ≪in such a way that pulling them apart would transform them into something else≫ (de la Cadena, 2015).

Both examples demonstrate how **language and grammar play a fundamental role in creating purpose and meaning, and how these can profoundly shape structures and affect thoughts and behaviours of different communities**.`[?2]`

## Beyond simulation / Technological delusion

In the previous chapters I have presented the dangers of techno-scientific hegemony and the consequences of binary thinking in general terms. I will try to elaborate further in the context of digital technologies.

In one of his last essays entitled *Transdisciplinarity Must Become Transversality*, Felix Guattari manifests a certain sympathy for technology, or at least he places some hope in it. Envisioning a future of transdisciplinarity strongly connected with the development of new technologies, the author states:

> In the first place, it will be possible to liberate a greater and greater quantity of activity and of labour from repetitive material tasks and to devote it to study, research and culture, which will establish new junctions of all kinds between themselves.`[<3]` […] In the second place,the evolution of informatics, its junction with television, telematics, data banks and the image, will develop a sort of machinic transdisciplinarity. This is already largely the case with every major discovery, every major technological innovation, which does not just irrigate neighbouring domains but frequently has fallout in the most distant of domains. […] In the third place, one might think that the era to come will lay down a profound transformation of the means of expression, knowledge, coordination and sensibility.
>
> *(Guattari, 1992)*

We can certainly understand the French intellectual's positions and justify his enthusiasm if we consider the historical context in which they were conceived, but we cannot ignore what has happened in the last thirty years to draw our very different conclusions. **At what point did an optimistic vision of the future transform into a narrative that promotes techno-scientific hegemony? Was there ever a transition, or are these simply two sides of the same coin?**

Many of the discourses on the latest technological developments seem not to take enough into account the philosophical`[?3]` transformation that such process implies. While a big part of the public discourse is concerned with political, ecological, social, ethical aspects—which are all extremely valid and urgent—, a better philosophical understanding might provide a solid foundation for developing long-term strategies. For example, the act of converting the sensible (images, sound, text, …) into digital formats implies a necessary reduction, compression and loss of information. While it might be tempting to ignore the parts we consider irrelevant and negligible (science does this all the time), sometimes these tiny details can have huge implications. Another argument could be that the human brain is constantly selecting and interpreting external inputs, and thus a reduction of information is necessary for our performing our living functions. But the machine still lies between us and the sensible, an invisible filter that predigests and preselects everything, and then our ability to digest and select becomes slowly dependent on that external entity.

The ever-growing pervasiveness of digital technologies in our lives has a fundamental problem: it reinforces a single perspective on reality. By being constantly exposed, and by compulsively translating every aspect of our life into digital form, we are convinced to believe that everything can be translated, that it is just a matter of technique and architecture. The belief that a human brain can be reproduced—not just simulated—together with the idea that any sensible or mental phenomenon can be reduced to a series of logical steps, implies that we can understand, control and manipulate virtually *anything*. But no matter how powerful, granular or accurate, the current information technology is based on one simple principle: divide and conquer. **+** The illusion of seamlesness represents a potential danger, as **it introduces a cognitive dissonance between what we perceive** (a smooth, ultra-detailed, closest-to-nature experience) **and what it actually is** (a ridiculous amount of numbers and operations that materialize as a copy of the sensible).

By questioning science and digital technologies, my aim is to reveal the boundaries, the limits and the axioms implicit in the discoveries and methods. > Suddenly, an echo from the first section:

**If it is not possible to convey the exact same meaning in the communication between two individuals, how can we expect effective communication to occur on a larger scale, across (trans-) disciplines?**

Ludwik Fleck will help us to elaborate on the problem from a language perspective. Similarly to Bill Readings, he theorizes the impossibility of a pure, *lossless* exchange of ideas from the perspective of *thought collectives*. It is not only a matter of socio-cultural friction between groups: language itself represents the main obstacle. *Thought styles* constrain ≪the individual by determining "what can be thought in no other way."≫. He continues: ≪Since all words bear a more or less distinctive coloring conforming to a given thought style, a character which changes during their passage from one collective to the next, they always undergo a certain change in their meaning as they circulate intercollectively. One could compare the meaning of the words “force,” “energy,” or “experiment” for a physicist, a philologist or a sportsman≫ (Fleck, 1935).

Dwelling on this apparent contradiction inherent in language, we can conclude that not all differences, oppositions, contrasts need to be resolved. **+** **Language is a means to define, to sharpen, to separate and—*at the same time*—an intangible, poetic, open-ended device**.

In a similar fashion, the notion of transdisciplinarity entails the dissolution of boundaries between the disciplines, while–*at the same time*–requiring them. A transdisciplinary process sacrifices the rigor required by the discipline in order to gain a *different type* of understanding. In this sense, transdisciplinarity does not present itself as a better substitute for estabilished disciplines; rather, it encourages a constant questioning and renegotiation of the discipline structures, as well as creating space for transformation and the emergence of new fields of inquiry.`[0]`

## Theory of models

> The scientific world is not a closed world, for which the well-known metaphor of the ivory tower stands, but it is – whether science likes it or not – an open world.
>
> – *Mittelstrass p.S70*

[ Not agreeing with this. In this terms “scientific world” is the world viewed by science, so it is in a way confined to the rules of science. I would argue that there are instead multiple worlds. Or there is only one sensible world, but multiple *models* of the world. By the term *model* I intend a set of epistemological assumptions and practices that exist for a given subject. The extent to which a model defines and incorporates the world is highly variable, and multiple *models* can coexist simultaneously. ]

I will try to provide a metaphor to describe how I intend the notion of *model*, and why it might prove useful. Science, philosophy, religion and theories in general can be viewed as structures (or *models*) that we use to understand or explain the world. We *project a model onto the world* we experience. Every *model* is grounded in language (or languages), and it requires a certain degree of approximation and compromise. Every system has boundaries, and axioms that provide the basic components required to build a system that makes sense. Though, it is not possible to explain a system from within. Boundaries can be extended, but they will be set further, never destroyed.

Speaking in geometric terms, each *model* aims to encapsulate the world as effectively as possible, within the constraints of its own structural limitations (basic shapes, rules, etc…)

Unconditional adherence to a single model, seen as the “true” or “only possible” description of reality, can be problematic. Firstly, because a description of reality cannot be identical to reality itself. And secondly, because although a model may work exceptionally well and suit a specific context, its partial effectiveness does not demonstrate its validity in absolute terms.

> When it was previously said that subjects and disciplines are not ‘natural’, but rather something that is historically constrained by the history of science, and whose boundaries are often not theoretical but historical, this does not mean that the significance of subjects, and especially of disciplines, should be underestimated. Rather, this should point to a systematic constitutive task beyond the assumption of realism. Only the fact that this task has been solved in various ways in scientific development and will probably be solved in a variety of ways in the future makes the historical character of the building of disciplines. The fundamental role of disciplinary forms of scientific knowledge remains unaffected, also under transdisciplinary aspects.
>
> *– Mittelstrass p.S74*

> Such a stylized solution, and there is always only one, is called truth. Truth is not "relative" and certainly not "subjective" in the popular sense of the word. lt is always, or almost always, completely determined within a thought style. One can never say that the same thought is true for A and false for B. lf A and B belong to the same thought collective, the thought will be either true or false for both. But if they belong to different thought collectives, it will just *not* be *the same thought*! lt must either be unclear to, or be understood differently by, one of them. *Truth is not a convention, but rather in historical perspective, an event in the history of thought, in its contemporary context, stylized thought constraint*.
>
> – Fleck p.100

> Words as such constitute a special medium of intercollective communication. Since all words bear a more or less distinctive coloring conforming to a given thought style, a character which changes during their passage from one collective to the next, they always undergo a certain change in their meaning as they circulate intercollectively.
>
> *– Fleck p.109*

Language and meaning are the foundation for models. They make a certain structures possible. It is not only about the words used and their definition, but also how is a certain meaning assigned, what is its effect and what are boundaries and relationships between words within a certain model.
